NatWest Bank: per-tenant egress for a multi-tenant Kubernetes platform

NatWest Bank was building the next generation of its multi-tenant platform on EKS, to run what the bank calls Important Business Services, which is the regulator's term for the services that must not fail. I joined the core platform team, mostly to work on tenant isolation and observability. This case study covers the first of those: the per-tenant egress solution I designed and built, so that tenants could reach the on-premise services they need without stepping on each other, and so that the platform team would know straight away when something was wrong. (The observability work has its own write-up.)

Situation

The tenants are the bank's own application teams, each of them with their own workloads, their own on-premise dependencies and their own view on how urgent their requests are. On a shared platform, egress is one of the places where tenants end up sharing more than they should. If everybody goes out through the same gateways, one tenant with a chatty application or a misbehaving retry loop can slow everybody else down; this is the well-known "noisy neighbour" problem.

In a bank there is a second problem on top of it: each tenant should only be able to reach the on-premise services it is actually allowed to reach, and this has to be provable. So the platform needed egress that was segregated per tenant, where each tenant could declare which on-premise services their applications need and where a failure in one tenant's egress would be detected quickly and would not affect anybody else.

Three things made this hard, in increasing order of difficulty:

  • Self-service. A design where the platform team has to hand-edit configuration every time a tenant wants to reach a new destination does not scale, and it makes the platform team the bottleneck. Tenants had to be able to configure their own egress, within limits set by the platform.
  • Knowing when it breaks. An egress gateway that is silently down is worse than one that is loudly down, because the tenant finds out before the platform team does.
  • Plain TCP. Istio is very comfortable with HTTP, but some of the on-premise services the tenants depend on use plain TCP protocols, and that traffic still had to be encrypted in transit and attributable to a tenant. Without a solution, those external plain TCP services were simply unreachable.

What I did

I designed, implemented and tested a solution based on Istio egress gateways, with one set of gateways per tenant. Each tenant gets its own gateway, so a problem with one tenant's egress stays with that tenant. The configuration is all in code and goes through the normal review process.

For self-service, I built a mechanism that lets tenants declare, in their own configuration, the on-premise services their applications are allowed to reach, without the platform team having to intervene. The platform team still have to approve the changes, so a tenant cannot simply do what it wants. Each change was approved by the relevant authoriser, and records of all changes were kept in order to pass the audit.

For detection, I wrote continuous health checks that exercise each gateway and alert the SRE team when one stops working. They test that traffic actually gets through the gateway, which tells you a lot more than whether its pod is running.

For plain TCP, I implemented a way of routing TCP traffic by encapsulating it in Istio mTLS, so that non-HTTP protocols get the same encryption and the same per-tenant segregation as everything else. Getting non-HTTP traffic to flow cleanly through Istio egress gateways was the technically tricky part of this work. I will spare you the details here, but it took a fair amount of reading of Istio's documentation and a fair amount of testing.

Result

The egress solution went into production and the tenants used it as intended. The platform passed its audit with per-tenant egress, and three tenants are using it. The platform has passed every audit without any major findings, and it is still running smoothly to this day.

The plain TCP encapsulation worked, which was not a given when I started. The health checks also do what they were meant to do: the SRE team finds out about a failing gateway from an alert, not from a tenant.

Per-tenant egress is not an exotic requirement, but it gets bolted on late and badly if it is not designed in. If you are building a multi-tenant Kubernetes platform in a regulated environment and egress is still "one NAT gateway and hope", it is worth designing it in properly before the auditors ask.

With hindsight, I built the gateways first and the health checks second, which is the natural order and the wrong one. The health checks are a small amount of work, and they are what turns an egress gateway from something you hope is working into something you know is working. If I were doing it again, I would write the checks first and run them against the gateways as I built them; I would have caught a couple of configuration mistakes earlier than I did.

I would also push for the self-service configuration to be part of the design from the very first conversation with the tenants, rather than something added once the gateways existed. Tenants who have been told they can declare their own destinations start thinking about what they need much earlier, and that saves everybody time.
